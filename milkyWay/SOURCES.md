## How to check a claim

1. Find the object in the atlas and open its panel.
2. Read the **Sources for this entry** list at the foot of the panel — those are the
   specific references behind that entry's numbers and its *Recently* block.
3. Cross-check against the tables below, which give the same references grouped by kind.

Peer-reviewed papers are preferred throughout. Institutional releases (ESO, NASA, NOIRLab,
STScI, Rubin, Subaru) are used where an observatory is the best summary of its own result,
and are labelled `release` so they can be weighed accordingly. Catalogue and archive entries
are labelled `archive` and are the provenance for orbital elements and designations, not for
interpretation.

## Where the positions come from

**Galactic scale.** Markers are projected from catalogue galactic longitude, latitude and
heliocentric distance at draw time:

```
x = -d · cos(b) · sin(l)
y = -26,700 + d · cos(b) · cos(l)        (light-years; Sun → Galactic Centre = 26,700 ly)
```

The painted spiral behind the markers is **procedural** — a seeded model of four
logarithmic arms, wound so the Sun falls in the gap between the Sagittarius–Carina arm
and the Perseus arm, which is where it actually sits. It is an illustration of structure,
not an image, and no marker is ever placed against it by eye.

Entries whose longitude/latitude is indicative rather than a measurement — the atlas says
so in the panel, and these should not be quoted as coordinates:

**Solar System.** The eight major planets use JPL's Keplerian elements for approximate
positions (epoch J2000, rates per Julian century, valid 1800–2050), solved through Kepler's

0.98331 au and its aphelion to 1.01670 au, and the Sun's apparent ecliptic longitude to
within a fraction of a degree.

**Radial compression.** In the Solar System view, screen radius defaults to the real
distance raised to the power 0.34, so Mercury at 0.39 au and the Oort Cloud at 100,000 au
can share one screen. The scale control switches this off. Body diameters are compressed
logarithmically and are never to scale against the orbits.
